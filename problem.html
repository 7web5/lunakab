02 / PROBLEM

From landing pad
to habitat

Once a ~40 ft cargo module lands on the lunar surface, it must be moved from the landing pad — kept away from the habitat to avoid plume contamination — to the supply building and docked to a pressurized port, without human EVA involvement wherever possible.

Module
~40 ft cargo module
Alternates
20 ft container + 4 ft pods
Geometry
Rectangular prisms or cylinders

02Client

  • NASA HUNCHKennedy Space Center
  • NASA RoboticsMarshall Habitation

03Requirements

  1. Handle a module mass equivalent in 1/6 g (may be scaled for an Earth demo)
  2. Navigate unprepared lunar regolith terrain
  3. Include a dust-exclusion coupling at the habitat dock (~1–2 m opening)
  4. Operate autonomously, with human override capability
  5. Use a solar + battery power architecture
  6. Require no human EVA for nominal transport operation

04Major constraints

  1. Regolith is abrasive and clings electrostatically — all seals and joints must account for it
  2. The plume blast zone means the module lands far from the habitat, so the robot must traverse a significant distance
  3. 1/6 g means lighter loads but also lower traction for wheeled or tracked systems
  4. The docking interface must seal to the habitat pressure boundary before the module hatch opens

05Key challenges

  1. Designing a gripper or cradle that handles an irregular cylindrical load
  2. Pushing the robot to butt against a straight surface (simulated airlock)
  3. Budgeting power for a long-distance traverse with a heavy load
  4. Avoiding tipping instability when carrying an elevated load in low gravity

06Missions

Basic operations M01 — M06

M01

Demonstrate that the robot can safely start, stop, turn, reverse, and travel from a simulated lunar lander across an uneven lunar surface. The robot must reach a designated staging area without striking the lander, cargo, or obstacles.

M02

The robot must locate a cargo pod at the landing site and position itself correctly for pickup. Students demonstrate controlled alignment, approach, and docking without manually repositioning either the robot or cargo.

M03

Retrieve one simulated 4 ft × 2 ft Astrobotic cargo pod and mechanically secure it for transportation. The robot must demonstrate that the load remains stable while starting, stopping, turning, and traversing uneven terrain.

M04

Carry the pod from the simulated landing zone to the supply-building/habitat area without dropping or damaging it. The course should include rocks, slopes, turns, loose-regolith simulation, and other obstacles representative of lunar surface transportation.

M05

At the habitat, the robot must accurately position and unload the cargo into a designated receiving zone. Success requires both transportation and controlled cargo placement — not simply dropping the payload near the building.

M06

Demonstrate an end-to-end mission: receive a cargo assignment, navigate to the lander, acquire the payload, transport it, deliver it to the correct destination, and return for another load. Students should measure mission time, distance traveled, energy consumed, positioning accuracy, and successful delivery.

Advanced / innovative M07 — M12

M07

Simulate the storyboard's 2 km separation between the landing pad and habitat, scaled appropriately for the prototype course. The robot must autonomously follow or determine a safe route while avoiding craters, rocks, restricted zones, steep terrain, and simulated landing-plume hazard areas.

M08

Develop an adaptable cargo-handling concept capable of working with the three logistics architectures in the lunar-base scenario: Astrobotic's smaller pods, a Blue Origin cylindrical cargo module, and a SpaceX-scale shipping-container concept. Prototypes need not lift full-scale representations; instead they should demonstrate a scalable interface, attachment system, or modular cargo-handling architecture.

M09

Two robots must work together to accomplish a task that one robot cannot efficiently complete alone — for example, moving an oversized habitat component, positioning cargo, clearing a route, or transferring a payload between vehicles. The robots should communicate their position, task status, cargo condition, and next action while preventing collisions.

M10

During the mission, judges introduce an unknown problem: a blocked road, failed sensor, communications loss, low battery, shifted cargo, disabled wheel, or unavailable receiving location. The robot must detect the problem and either autonomously select a safe alternative or communicate enough diagnostic information for a remote operator to make a decision.

M11

Instead of telling the robot exactly what to do, give the system several cargo requests with different priorities — habitat supplies, replacement batteries, life-support equipment, scientific instruments, and emergency repair parts. The software must determine what should move first, which robot should move it, which route to use, and when the mission should occur, then demonstrate the resulting plan with the prototype.

M12

An open-ended challenge: "What capability will a lunar cargo robot need that NASA hasn't asked you to demonstrate yet?" Teams identify the problem and demonstrate their own innovation — self-charging, robotic trailer trains, automatic cargo identification, cooperative lifting, regolith-clearing attachments, predictive maintenance, autonomous emergency resupply, swappable tools, temporary road construction, or using cargo containers as structural components of the lunar base.
